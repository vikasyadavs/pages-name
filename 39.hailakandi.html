Pre-British Era
Agrarian Roots & Kuki Settlements
The name "Hailakandi" itself echoes its agricultural soul — derived either from the Kuki words meaning "small state with temporary farmland" or from Sailkandi, a prized paddy variety cultivated abundantly here. Rice cultivation, hand-loom weaving, and fishing along the Barak River formed the backbone of livelihoods for generations.
1857 — 1869
The Sepoy Mutiny & British Administration
Hailakandi's people displayed extraordinary courage at Ron Tila, Mohanpur against British soldiers. On June 1, 1869, the district was formally constituted as a civil sub-division. British rule brought the first roads and railway lines — primarily to transport tea and paper mill produce — fundamentally changing the economic geography.
Late 1800s — 1940s
Tea Garden Economy Blossoms
The British established large tea gardens across the region, creating the district's first organized industrial employment. Notable managers like James Marshall Winchester and Mac Farlane Chalmers shaped both the landscape and local institutions — the Chalmers Higher Secondary School in Katlicherra still carries that legacy. By the turn of the century, over 55 sq. km was under tea cultivation.
1956 — 1989
Paper Mill, Education & Independence
The landmark Hindustan Paper Mill at Panchgram became a cornerstone employer, offering hundreds of formal industrial jobs. Hailakandi College (affiliated to Gauhati University in 1956) seeded an educated workforce. On September 29, 1989, Hailakandi officially became the 24th district of Assam, triggering fresh government recruitment and infrastructure investment.
2006 — Present
Aspirational District & Digital Awakening
Designated an Aspirational District in 2006, Hailakandi began receiving targeted BRGF development funds. MGNREGA, PMEGP, and NRLM schemes opened new employment avenues for rural households. Digital literacy programs and improved connectivity are now attracting the first wave of services-sector startups to this once-isolated valley district.
Employment Evolution

How Jobs Have
Evolved Here

From subsistence farming to organised industry, and now tentative digital growth — Hailakandi's employment story spans three distinct economic chapters.

1870s – 1950s
Colonial Agriculture & Tea Labour
The dominant employer was the land itself. Over 50% of residents worked in paddy cultivation, while British-owned tea gardens absorbed thousands of daily-wage workers. Artisans — weavers, potters, and bamboo craftspeople — sustained local village economies. Formal employment was rare and largely gatekept by colonial administration.
Agriculture Tea
1960s – 1990s
Government & Paper Mill Dominance
Post-independence, government jobs (teachers, clerks, police, forest officials) became aspirational career goals. The Hindustan Paper Mill became Panchgram's identity, offering stable manufacturing employment. Railway and road maintenance brought semi-skilled blue-collar work. Private enterprise remained thin — mostly small traders and tea estate contractors.
Government Manufacturing
2000s – 2015
Scheme Economy & Informal Growth
MGNREGA and rural livelihood missions created seasonal employment safety nets. Small retail, transport, and construction work grew organically. NGO and healthcare sectors expanded, driven by government health missions. However, manufacturing stagnated and many educated youth began migrating to Silchar, Guwahati, and beyond for private-sector careers.
MGNREGA NGO Sector
2016 – Present
Digital Transition & Services Rising
Today, 78% of registered enterprises are in services. Mobile retail, digital finance, logistics, and healthcare staffing are new job generators. The district's educated youth increasingly return from metros with skills. E-governance has created data-entry and digital-services roles. Agro-processing and handicraft export remain underexplored but high-potential sectors.
Services Digital
